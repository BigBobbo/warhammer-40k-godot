The 40k AI — how it works, and what it should become

The AI opponent is a large hand-built scoring engine. It is better than its reputation, and it has a measurement lab most game studios never build. But its structure caps how good it can get. This page explains the current system, the target system, and the one decision the owner has to make. The step-by-step plan lives in .llm/ai-overhaul-todo.md.

Summary

  • Today the AI plays by scoring candidate actions and picking the highest, one action at a time, with hand-set weights. Your description of it is accurate.
  • Its real ceiling is structural: 78% of its scoring arithmetic is hardcoded numbers no tuning process can reach, most decisions leave no trace in the data, it never looks ahead, and the phases do not share one plan or one definition of "a good position".
  • A serious self-play measurement lab already exists in the repo (deterministic AI-vs-AI games, a paired evaluator, an optimizer, acceptance gates). It has honestly measured two plausible improvement ideas as useless and one as harmful. It is starved, not broken.
  • The target: a three-layer AI (turn-level strategist → per-phase tacticians → bounded lookahead) over one board-value function, with personas as data — wrapped in an automated improvement loop (propose → play → measure → gate → ship): a Karpathy loop for the AI itself.
  • Do not rewrite from scratch. The evidence — including a decade of results from Blood Bowl AI competitions — favors restructuring the existing engine around shared services, then letting the loop improve it.
  • Flagged decision: static AI vs. one that improves over time. Recommendation: offline-improvable — the AI stays deterministic and explainable in the player's hands; the improvement happens between releases, machine-driven, human-gated. Details in §11.
PART I

The AI today

§1The shape of it

Three components run the AI. AIPlayer.gd (4,022 lines) is the driver: it watches game signals, waits a beat, and asks for a decision. AIDecisionMaker.gd (22,553 lines, 390 static functions) is the brain: it takes a snapshot of the game state and a list of legal action types, and returns exactly one action. AIAbilityAnalyzer.gd reads unit abilities into numeric multipliers. A difficulty config gates features per level.

The AI submits its action through NetworkIntegration.route_action() — the same pipeline a human's clicks go through. The engine validates every action and rejects illegal ones. This is a real strength: the AI cannot cheat, and rule changes cannot silently desynchronize it. Then the loop repeats, 400–750 times per game.

§2What "ranking model" means here

Your description holds: at each step the AI enumerates candidates, computes a score for each, and takes the best. A charge decision, for example, multiplies expected melee damage, target value, the exact probability of making the 2d6 charge distance (all 36 outcomes enumerated), overwatch risk, and several bonuses — then compares the best score against a threshold built from tempo, difficulty, round strategy, army archetype and faction aggression.

The weights live in three layers. About 128 named parameters are read through get_param(), which means a config file, a per-player profile, or a conditional rule ("if losing by 10 VP in round 4, raise aggression") can override them without touching code — there are two web tools for exactly this. But roughly 170 more coefficients are bare literals in the code (score += 3.0), and those outnumber the reachable ones inside the functions that decide games: reserves scoring, objective assignment, embarkation, fight-target selection. A tuning process — human or machine — cannot touch them, no matter how many games it plays. This is your "if a decision is not encoded in the weights, it never improves" concern, and the repo's own audit confirmed it mechanically.

§3What it already does well

  • Army-wide shooting optimization. The shooting phase builds a weapon×target expected-damage matrix over every shooter at once and allocates greedily by marginal value, with kill-threshold and overkill logic. This is genuine cross-unit coordination.
  • Mission-aware play. Objectives are priced in expected victory points from the actual mission cards, with marginal analysis ("does one more objective reach the hold-2 threshold?"). Secondary missions shape movement, target choice and discards through ~20 per-card assessors.
  • Cross-phase glue exists — eight caches: a persistent movement battle plan, charge intents and lock-targets computed at movement start, shooting that deliberately under-shoots planned charge targets, gang-up and finish-off coordination in melee.
  • It explains itself. Every decision narrates chosen vs. rejected options with scores into the game log, with board-linked hover arrows. Players can read why. This transparency is a product feature worth protecting.
  • It is honest about failure. Six implemented-then-measured features are kill-switched to zero in the code with their measured confidence intervals in comments.

§4The measurement lab (already built)

Since July the repo has grown something rare: an evaluation pipeline for the AI itself. Headless AI-vs-AI games run deterministically (seeded dice, seeded decks, seeded AI noise — two runs at the same seed produce identical games). Every game writes a record with every decision considered, every score, provenance hashes, and the outcome; a season converts to a queryable database. A paired evaluator answers "is candidate B better than baseline A?" with side-swapped, seed-paired arms and a pre-registered stopping rule. A cross-entropy optimizer searches parameter space. A five-gate acceptance check (two matchups, no stalls, no scenario regressions, an action-mix "still looks like 40k" guard, interpretability caps) decides shippability.

It has already produced the most valuable thing a lab can produce: honest negative results.

§5Why it caps out — five walls

  1. Most decisions are invisible. Only ~5 decision types emit records. Shooting records the chosen plan, not the alternatives it weighed; movement records one number instead of its breakdown. Offline analysis — or any learning — cannot study what was never written down.
  2. Most scoring is unreachable. 78% of scoring arithmetic uses bare literals no profile, config, or optimizer can move. Tuning today searches a sixth of the machine.
  3. It never looks ahead. No search, no simulation, no "what does the opponent do to this position?" — the code has expected-value dice math but zero lookahead. The Competitive difficulty's use_look_ahead flag is dead config: nothing calls it.
  4. The phases do not share one goal. Eight side-caches coordinate pairwise by convention. There is no board evaluation — no function that says how good a position is — so each phase optimizes its own local proxy. Five stacked multipliers (faction, archetype, round, tempo, noise) modify scores multiplicatively with no calibration. And the multi-phase plan only builds at Hard difficulty and above; on Normal, cross-phase play is silently off.
  5. Improvement is starved. A game costs 48 s (elite mirror) to 487 s (horde mirror); per-seed noise is 9–15 VP; proving a +4 VP change needs hundreds of games. The one optimizer campaign so far ran a tenth of its designed budget. Nothing runs in CI. The loop exists but turns slowly.

§6Should we rip it out?

No — and this is a conclusion from evidence, not sentiment. The brain's nominal interface is one function, but its real surface is wide: fourteen reactive entry points (overwatch, rerolls, interventions…), some twenty geometry helpers called directly by other systems, and the narration contract the UI and tests depend on. Meanwhile, well over half of the file is not decision logic at all — it is reusable geometry, collision resolution, probability math and weapon parsing that any engine would need. Rewriting all of that buys nothing.

The outside world agrees. The closest research analog to this game is Blood Bowl — a Games Workshop tabletop game with dice, a board, and a brutal branching factor, which has run an academic AI competition since 2019. Its standing result: scripted bots built on domain knowledge beat pure machine-learning approaches for years; the first ML winner (2021) had to imitate a scripted bot and hybridize with search to get there; full-game tree search alone struggles. The path that wins at this scale is: keep the domain knowledge, restructure it, add selective search, and measure relentlessly.

PART II

The target AI

§7Design goals

  • Stronger: beats today's frozen AI by a margin a player can feel (measured, not felt).
  • Improvable: every decision visible in data, every coefficient reachable by tuning, and an automated loop that converts games played into strength gained.
  • Explainable: the game log keeps narrating true reasons. This is a product promise.
  • Characterful: armies play differently through data-defined personas, not code forks.
  • Same hardware: milliseconds per decision in-engine; no ML runtime in the game.

§8The architecture: three layers, two shared services

The user's instinct in the brief is exactly right: per-phase solvers without a connecting layer cannot express a strategy. The target adds the connecting layer explicitly — and keeps the per-phase knowledge that already works.

  • Strategist. Once per turn, at every difficulty, build a TurnPlan: which objectives matter this round (from the mission math that already exists), a task per unit (hold, take, screen, hunt, deliver, hold-lane), a deployment posture, and a CP budget that can hold a command point back for overwatch. The eight ad-hoc caches become views of this one object. The plan persists, is consumed as units act, replans only for cause, and is narrated — the July coordination work already proved this pattern in the movement phase.
  • Tacticians. The per-phase solvers stay — they hold real domain knowledge. They change in two ways: candidates are generated against the TurnPlan's tasks, and scored with named, reachable, recorded terms instead of inline literals.
  • Lookahead. A forward model applies expected-value outcomes to a lightweight state view — no dice sampling needed for evaluation, because the combat math is already expectation-based. Spend it only where it pays: the opponent's best reply to the top few high-stakes moves, and beam search over charge-declaration and fight-activation order, where branching is small. Search depth becomes the difficulty knob, replacing score noise.
  • V(state). One function says how good a position is: projected primary and secondary VP over the remaining scoring turns, material expected value, threat balance — decomposed into named terms, every term tunable. It ships dark first: computed and recorded for a season, validated against actual outcomes (does round-3 V predict the final margin?), and only then allowed to steer decisions. The scoring-horizon failure taught this order.
  • Personas. A persona is a data file: parameter pack + conditional rules (both exist today) + strategist biases (new: risk posture, reserve appetite, posture preference, target priorities). Ork "Green Tide" and Custodes "Shield Host" ship first. Difficulty stops meaning "how much random noise" and starts meaning "how deep the plan and the search go".

§9The improvement loop — a Karpathy loop for the game AI

Karpathy's loop, in its 2026 form: an agent proposes a change, an automated harness evaluates it against a baseline, improvements are kept, failures are rolled back, and the committed result becomes the next baseline. The model never retrains; the artifacts improve. That is precisely the right shape here, and the repo is already more than half way to it.

What makes this loop trustworthy rather than a treadmill:

  • Determinism. Same seed, same game — so a no-op candidate measures exactly 0.00, and any claimed "behavior-preserving" refactor can be proven byte-identical.
  • Tactical exams. Full games are noisy (9–15 VP per seed). Constructed positions with a known best play ("take the free objective", "screen the deep strike", "finish the wounded Knight") give sharp pass/fail signal in seconds. Chess engines have used test suites this way for decades; this project's exam suite becomes the cheap inner loop.
  • The Goodhart guard. The gate already checks that a candidate still charges, still fights, still plays like 40k. It gets widened into a behavioral fingerprint with drift limits calibrated from A/A noise, plus a human skim of two full game logs per shipped change.
  • Everything proposes through the same gate — a human's hunch, the optimizer's parameter vector, an LLM's profile rule, an LLM's code patch. There are no side doors.

§10What the research supports

  • Bot Bowl (Blood Bowl AI competition, IEEE CoG): scripted domain knowledge beat pure RL for years; the first ML winner imitated a scripted bot; full-game MCTS remains impractical. Closest analog to 40k in existence. Validates: keep the scripted knowledge, add selective search, measure hard.
  • XCOM 2 (GDC): utility scoring over generated move candidates with named factors — the same family as this codebase; its strength came from disciplined factor design, not exotic algorithms.
  • Civilization VI: leader personalities as data-defined weight sets over one engine — the persona model proposed here.
  • Utility + search hybrids (e.g. the Tactical Troops study): utility AI assigns orders at the strategic layer, search refines tactics below — the two-layer split in Fig. 2.
  • Stochastic search theory: expectimax and determinized/chance-node MCTS handle dice cleanly; with expectation-based combat math, 1-ply evaluation needs no sampling at all.
  • AlphaEvolve (DeepMind, 2025) and Karpathy's loop (2026): LLM proposes, an automated evaluator disposes, improvements compound — shown to improve real production heuristics. This is §9's blueprint, applied to profile rules first and code patches second.

§11The decision you asked to be flagged: static vs. improving

Decision required — improvement posture

OptionWhat it meansStrength ceilingCost / risk
A. Static Restructure (Parts II §8), tune by hand, ship. No automated loop beyond one optimizer pass. One-time jump, then plateau. Every future gain costs an engineer-day per hypothesis. Lowest. But the measured record shows hand hypotheses usually fail (−2.05, −4.29).
B. Offline-improvable ← recommended Same restructuring, plus the §9 loop on a cadence: optimizer + LLM proposers, human-gated, shipping versioned profiles between releases. The shipped AI stays deterministic and explainable. Compounding. Strength grows while the team sleeps; failures roll back with numbers. Moderate: lab upkeep + compute (hundreds of games per accepted change). Goodhart risk — mitigated by the fingerprint gate and exams.
C. Learned components Add trained models (a fitted value function, then policy nets) on top of B. Highest in theory. Not now: 200–500 games/day is orders of magnitude short of RL budgets, no ML runtime in-engine, and it trades away explainability. Bot Bowl says it loses at this scale.

Recommendation: B. The distinction that matters: the AI a player faces is static within a release — deterministic, testable, explainable — and improving across releases, with the loop doing the searching and humans holding the gate. B strictly contains A (stop the loop and you have A), and B builds the two things C would ever need (a decision dataset and a forward model), so it forecloses nothing. Revisit C only if B plateaus and the dataset has grown to millions of decisions.

The plan is sequenced so this decision is not urgent: Phases 1–2 (visibility, reachability, measurement capacity, the value function) are identical under A and B. The checkpoint sits at the end of Phase 2.

§12What we would not do

  • Per-action LLM play. 50–200 engine actions per turn at ~1–3 s per call means minutes-long turns, dollars per game, no determinism, and a model that can still propose illegal moves. Bounded roles instead: strategist-level nudges (a few calls per game, optional) and the offline proposer roles in §9.
  • Deep RL / AlphaZero-style self-play training. Needs millions of episodes; this project generates hundreds per day. See option C.
  • A ground-up rewrite. §6. The plan restructures in place, behavior-preserving at every step the evaluator can verify.

§15How we will know it worked

  • The loop's falsifiable metric (inherited from the lab's design brief): a fully automated cycle produces a change beating the incumbent by ≥ +4 VP per game at two standard errors across ≥ 2 matchups, without regressing the scenario suite. That has never happened yet; the plan exists to make it routine.
  • The ladder: margin against the frozen 2026-08 baseline rises release over release — absolute progress, not progress against a moving target.
  • The exams: pass count on the tactical suite rises; aspirational exams flip on as WS-C/WS-D land.
  • The product: two personas a player can tell apart in two rounds; a difficulty ladder where each step measurably beats the one below; a game log that still explains every choice.
