# research:commercial-practice

## Narrative

HEADLINE: Across every commercial digital adaptation of a tabletop wargame I could find, no studio has shipped a symmetric, full-ruleset wargame AI that reliably challenges experienced players on decision quality alone. The universal industry pattern is: a competent-but-exploitable heuristic AI, plus material/statistical handicaps at higher difficulties. Your constraint (no dice cheating) is actually industry-normal — commercial games almost never fudge dice against the player (several secretly fudge dice FOR the player); they buy difficulty with points, stats, and resources.

1. OFFICIAL 40K DIGITAL GAMES. Battlesector (Black Lab): reviewers called the enemy AI "primitive" and the strategy simple (Digitally Downloaded), Steam/Slitherine forums describe skirmish AI as "zerg rushing" and "brain dead"; its hardest difficulty is stat inflation — enemies get roughly +1/3 HP, +15 accuracy, +10 crit, and the player fields 80% of normal points (reported, Steam thread + PCGamesN difficulty overhaul article). Gladius (Proxy Studios/Slitherine): the cleanest data point — a developer confirmed in a Steam thread I fetched that difficulty is a "Loyalty" bonus in WorldParameters.xml (Easy 0, Medium +5, Hard +15, Very Hard +30, Impossible +50; each point = 2% resource gain and production speed), and stated verbatim: "having them cheat by giving them extra Loyalty currently is a necessary evil to create a challenging experience." This from a studio whose earlier game Pandora was praised for AI. Sanctus Reach: mixed — one reviewer found the campaign tight, users call the AI "awful" and trivially exploited (e.g. Land Speeders). Armageddon (Panzer Corps lineage): reviewers note AI settings deliberately DIMINISH AI effectiveness so new players can learn — difficulty is tuned down, not up. Mechanicus: AI "behaves erratically"; difficulty is player-facing sliders on enemy health/damage/economy. Chaos Gate — Daemonhunters: challenge comes from encounter design and stat scaling, with community complaints of "balance completely out of whack" and AI issues patched for months; once players outscale, the AI cannot compensate. Tacticus (Snowprint): in async PvP the defending roster is played by the AI and community guides state flatly the AI is "very, very bad and dumb" — the business model tolerates this because attackers WANT weak defense AI. Necromunda: Underhive Wars (Rogue Factor, closest to an actual GW skirmish tabletop): AI described in reviews as "overwhelmingly stupid," units running into walls and self-grenading — a commercial and critical failure driven substantially by AI. Pattern: not one official 40k adaptation is praised for AI decision quality; every one that is challenging gets there via numbers.

2. BLOOD BOWL — THE COMMERCIAL TWIN OF BOTBOWL. Cyanide has shipped three generations of Blood Bowl with a full commercial budget and license, and the AI is the perennial weak point. BB2: "in the single-player campaign the AI is simply pathetic"; it doesn't try to score; players report Hard AI plays WORSE than Normal because its constrained "best-odds" policy is degenerate. BB3: NME's review and beta coverage call the AI self-sabotaging (e.g. choosing a Skull result over strictly better dice); devs publicly apologized for the launch but never claimed a strong AI. On the research side (Wargamer article I fetched, plus the MimicBot arXiv paper): Bot Bowl has run since 2019 at IEEE CoG; the 2022 winner (Drefsante) was a SCRIPTED bot; the best ML bots could not beat the scripted bot at all and could not even achieve a perfect record against a RANDOM agent; the random baseline scored seven draws including one against the tournament winner. Justesen's paper puts Blood Bowl's per-turn branching at ~10^30 (chess ~30, Go ~300), some estimates 10^50. This is the closest existing analogue to your problem (dice, IGOUGO, tabletop-faithful, spatial) and both the commercial and academic branches failed to produce a strong AI after years of effort. Your 40k game has a larger continuous state/action space than Blood Bowl.

3. TABLETOP-FAITHFUL CASES WITH RESPECTED AI — WHAT ACTUALLY EXISTS. (a) Field of Glory II (Byzantine Games/Slitherine; direct port of Richard Bodley Scott's tabletop miniatures rules): the best symmetric case. Reviews call the AI "surprisingly competent," forming battle lines, using terrain, kiting with archers. But the Steam AI thread I fetched is sobering: experienced players say "the AI will oblige by attacking into whatever well laid trap you set," it drifts piecemeal into concentrations, and higher difficulties give it ~25% more troops (Emperor) rather than better decisions. So the genre-best symmetric miniatures AI = competent baseline, loses to any experienced player on even points, difficulty = points handicap. (b) Second Front (Jo Bader, ASL-inspired): reviews call the AI "an absolute monster," flanking guns, hiding tanks — reported, small squad-scale scenarios, largely PvE scenario play; I could not verify how much scenario scripting carries it. (c) Card/economic games: Race for the Galaxy (Keldon Jones/Temple Gates) uses a TD-learning neural net trained by self-play (30k+ games; GDC talk "A Neural Network in Production") and is widely considered genuinely strong without cheating; Through the Ages (CGE) AI is praised as fun as humans. These work because the action space is small and discrete and a game simulates in milliseconds — neither property holds for continuous-movement 40k on your 4-core, 1-8-min-per-game budget. (d) Co-op adaptations (Gloomhaven, Spirit Island): the "AI" is the board game's own rules — monster ability cards and invader phases are scripted BY DESIGN; digital just executes them. Respected, but it is design-as-AI, not decision-making. (e) Asymmetric competitive adaptations: Root (Dire Wolf) bots "err on the easier side," veterans quickly outwit them, and Dire Wolf's answer was to ship the Clockwork expansion — Leder's tabletop automa bots — as an alternative to their AI; Scythe digital has three AI levels and players asked for the tabletop Automa because the hard AI is beatable; Twilight Struggle's Playdek AI is weak enough that BGG's standing thread is "Why hasn't the digital version seen an improved AI yet?". Tabletop Simulator, of course, ships no AI at all. So: zero precedent for a respected symmetric wargame AI at 40k complexity; the respected cases are either simpler discrete games amenable to self-play RL, rules-scripted co-op opponents, or FoG2's "competent + points handicap."

4. TECHNIQUES. XCOM (Firaxis, GDC 2013 "AI Postmortems"): utility-scored movement maps — each candidate position scored on distance, flanking, cover, proximity to allies, visible enemies, plus per-archetype weights. That is architecturally the SAME single-ply weighted-heuristic design as your AIDecisionMaker, and it ships in the genre's most celebrated title — but inside an asymmetric PvE game where pod design, stat scaling, and outnumbering do the difficulty work, and where the game secretly fudges dice FOR the player below Legend (XCOM 2: +10/+15 aim per consecutive miss on shots ≥50%, capped at 95% — in config files, community-documented). Dave Mark's Infinite Axis Utility System plus tactical influence maps (deployed at ArenaNet, GDC 2015) is the industry-standard spatial-reasoning upgrade — influence maps are the established answer to exactly your deployment/screening/threat-projection weaknesses. Combat Mission (Battlefront): the proven full-fat wargame architecture is HUMAN-AUTHORED AI PLANS — scenario designers paint map zones and author timed/triggered order groups per scenario, with a reactive TacAI handling only local behavior; there is effectively no autonomous operational AI. That is precisely the "pre-described mission/board/army game plans" you're already open to, and it's how the most respected hardcore tactical wargame series has worked for 20 years. Civilization (Soren Johnson, "Game AI & Our Cheatin' Hearts," which I fetched): "artificial intelligence often needs to cheat just to be able to compete with the player" in symmetric games; players tolerate steady, transparent, incremental bonuses and despise information cheats and hidden instant rewards; "when the question is one of fairness, the player is always right." Civ VI Deity: ~+80% production/gold via cost discounts, +32% science/culture, +4 combat, extra starting units. Total War Legendary: hidden battle-stat bonuses; CA later made them removable after backlash. Battle for Wesnoth (open source): the wiki's own scenario-balancing guidance is to tune AI GOLD/INCOME, and warns that too much AI gold demands perfect play — a 20-year-old open-source project institutionalized "weak AI + resources" as the balancing lever.

5. THE NON-DICE HANDICAP MENU actually used in shipped games: points/army-size handicap (FoG2 +25% troops; Battlesector player at 80% points); stat inflation (Battlesector HP/accuracy/crit; Total War melee stats); economy multipliers (Gladius Loyalty; Civ discounts; Wesnoth gold); free/extra starting assets (Civ settlers/warriors); player-cost inflation (Battlesector upgrade prices); aggression/behavior retuning (BB2's Hard-worse-than-Normal is the cautionary tale); scripted scenario plans and triggers (Combat Mission, Wesnoth campaigns); tabletop automa rules shipped as the opponent (Root Clockwork); and dice fudging FOR the player at low difficulty (XCOM), the inverse of your red line.

6. BAR-SETTING. Is there ANY precedent for a full-fat wargame AI that regularly challenges experienced players without material handicaps? On the evidence: no. The best symmetric case (FoG2) is explicitly beatable by anyone who sets traps and is propped up by +25% troops at high difficulty. The nearest problem-twin (Blood Bowl) failed commercially (Cyanide, three attempts) and academically (Bot Bowl: scripted > ML, random draws vs winner). Studios accept this because (my inference from the pattern) the economics don't reward it: single-player wargame audiences accept "competent + handicap options," difficulty sliders are cheap and transparent, and the marginal buyer churns off frustration, not off AI exploitability discovered after 30 hours. Note the direction of every studio's solution when their AI fell short — Dire Wolf shipped automa rules; Battlefront shipped human-authored plans; Proxy shipped economy multipliers and called it a necessary evil; Firaxis shipped asymmetry and player-favoring dice. Nobody solved it with better search or learning inside a shipped spatial wargame.

## Implications

(1) Recalibrate the goal. The evidence says 'AI that outplays experienced humans on even terms' has no shipped precedent in this genre — not from Cyanide with a AAA license, not from Slitherine's 20-year stable, not from academia on the strictly-smaller Blood Bowl problem. Your CEM null results and heuristic ceiling are the normal industry outcome, not a project failure. The achievable, precedented target is: 'competent, non-embarrassing, non-exploitable-in-obvious-ways AI' + 'transparent material handicaps the player opts into'. (2) Your no-dice-cheating constraint costs you nothing — the industry doesn't cheat dice against players either. The standard menu is fully open to you and maps directly onto your one proven win-type (the reserves-cap change was a strategic/material-shaped fix): points handicap (AI plays 2,100-2,300 pts vs your 2,000 — FoG2's Emperor is ~+25%), curated harder army lists per fixture, CP/stratagem economy bonuses, and re-roll-style resource grants (a rules-native mechanic, not a fudge). These are cheap, measurable with your existing lab, and their effect sizes (a whole extra unit) will actually clear your ±3-4 VP detection floor, unlike decision-quality tweaks. (3) The Combat Mission precedent directly endorses your instinct for pre-authored deployment and mission plans: the most respected tactical wargame series ships human-authored per-scenario plans plus a reactive tactical layer, full stop. Given your own data (deployment decides rounds 1-2; 50% of move destinations dominated), hand-authoring deployment templates and per-mission macro plans (Root-Clockwork/automa style: 'earmark squads to rush mid') attacks your largest measured weaknesses with the technique the industry actually uses — and is far more likely to move VP than another parameter search. (4) If you invest in decision quality at all, the two precedented upgrades for your exact architecture are influence maps for the spatial layer (Dave Mark/ArenaNet; addresses your dominated-move-destination problem inside the existing utility framework) and fixing degenerate policy interactions (the BB2 'Hard plays worse than Normal' failure is a warning for profile tuning). Do NOT budget for self-play RL: the games where it worked (Race for the Galaxy) simulate in milliseconds with tiny discrete action spaces; at 1-8 min/game on 4 cores you cannot generate training data at 4+ orders of magnitude below what TD-Gammon-style methods need, and Bot Bowl's ML entries lost to scripts anyway. (5) 'Not worth pursuing' is the wrong frame; 'stop pursuing decision-quality parity and ship FoG2-style difficulty' is the right one. Concretely: keep the current engine as the baseline, add authored deployment/mission plans, and expose labeled difficulty tiers built on points/CP handicaps — that combination has commercial precedent (FoG2, Battlesector, Gladius) and is honest with the player, and Soren Johnson's research says players accept exactly this kind of steady, transparent bonus while 'challenging AI with no handicap' remains an unfilled promise industry-wide.
