# research:search-based-wargame-ai

## Narrative

The central question — could bounded lookahead beat your single-ply heuristic engine — decomposes into: does search work at this game scale at all, what did the field do when it didn't, what does stochasticity cost, and what does a slow forward model kill. The literature is unusually clear, and mostly sobering.

**1. Vanilla MCTS is empirically dead at multi-action board-game scale.** The closest published cousin to your problem is Blood Bowl. Justesen et al.'s challenge paper (CoG 2019) computes a turn-wise branching factor of ~10^50 (11 pieces, each moving several squares, grid board — strictly *smaller* than 40k's 40-80 models in continuous 2D) and states flatly that turn-based multi-action games at this branching factor "cause the breakdown of standard tree search methods, including MCTS." This is not just theory: the official botbowl MCTS tutorial reports that with 5 seconds per decision, MCTS manages only 361 iterations on the full 11-player board and achieves a 10% win rate against a *purely random* opponent (0.1 TDs/game; the remainder are presumably scoreless draws — my inference). On a 1-player toy board it gets 95%. That is the measured cliff between toy scale and real scale, in a game smaller and more discrete than yours. The Bot Bowl competition arc confirms it: scripted GrodBot won Bot Bowl I; the MCTS entry (Dryad) placed 3rd in Bot Bowl III; the first bot to beat scripted bots was MimicBot — imitation-learning a *scripted* bot, then RL fine-tuning — not search. Your botbowl worry is calibrated correctly in one direction (search/learning approaches struggled for years) but note the baseline fact: a decent scripted bot beats random 10-0 with 3.5 TDs/game. The failure mode is "can't beat good scripted play," not "loses to random" — for well-engineered scripted bots.

**2. What the field did instead: search over scripts, not actions — proven only on toys.** The RTS literature spent a decade on exactly your problem. Ontañón's Combinatorial Multi-Armed Bandit work (NaiveMCTS/naïve sampling, AIIDE 2013 / JAIR 2017) factors a multi-unit turn into per-unit bandits and demonstrably beats standard sampling as branching grows — but in microRTS, with trivially cheap forward models and 8x8–16x16 maps, and later portfolio methods beat it anyway. Barriga/Stanescu/Buro's Puppet Search (TCIAIG 2017) is the most relevant idea for you: take existing scripts, expose a small number of discrete choice points, and run adversarial look-ahead over *those* — it matched other agents on small maps and won on large ones, precisely because raw-action search dies at scale. Moraes & Lelis's asymmetric action abstraction (AAAI 2018) refines this: search finely over a few "important" units, script the rest. Yang & Ontañón's experimental survey (IEEE ToG 2021) consolidates the field's verdict: at scale, abstraction/script-space methods dominate un-abstracted search, though un-abstracted search is better where it can afford to run. Critically, Moraes's Nested-Greedy paper shows plain *greedy* search over abstractions is competitive with adversarial search in several settings — i.e., even in the flagship domain for this technique, the measured gap between 1-ply-greedy-over-scripts and deeper search is often small. The Stratega framework (Dockhorn, Perez-Liebana et al., AIIDE 2020) was built in C++ explicitly because statistical forward planning (MCTS/RHEA) needs a *fast* forward model; Elastic MCTS (CoG 2022) adds state abstraction and reports beating MCTS baselines with 10x smaller trees — again on Stratega scenarios, i.e., toy tactical maps. RHEA's wins over MCTS (Gaina et al.) are in GVGAI with ~40ms budgets and microsecond forward models. **Nothing in this literature has been demonstrated on a game with 40k's rules complexity, model count, or forward-model cost.** Transfer is a research bet, not an engineering task.

**3. Stochasticity: the one genuinely good news item.** 40k dice are analytically tractable — hit/wound/save/damage are binomial chains and charge distance is 2D6, so a 1-2 ply expectimax can use closed-form expected values and tail probabilities instead of sampled chance nodes or determinization. No cheating required, no sampling noise. The stochastic-game literature (Ballard's *-minimax; Hauk & Buro's "*-Minimax Performance in Backgammon") shows chance nodes brutally limit useful depth (backgammon's effective branching with chance is ~400), and the celebrated counter-example is TD-Gammon: a *strong evaluation function at 1-ply* reached human-expert level, and 2-3 ply on top of that strong eval gave superhuman play. The pattern that works in high-stochasticity games is exactly "greedy/shallow + strong eval," not deep search. Junghanns & Schaeffer's diminishing-returns work in chess adds the other half: the marginal value of extra depth depends on evaluation quality. Andy Jones's "Scaling Scaling Laws with Board Games" (2021) shows train-time and test-time compute trade off smoothly — but with a *learned, strong* evaluator. Searching deeper with a weak evaluator amplifies its noise. My inference, consistent with your own measurements (damage-math corrections changed zero decisions; CEM found nothing; macro terms dominate): bolting 2-ply search onto the current evaluation function would most likely measure ~0 VP, at 10-100x the compute — the same null your CEM run produced, for the same reason. Your measured "~50% of movement decisions dominated" is a candidate-generation and evaluation defect, not a depth defect; search cannot fix choosing among bad candidates scored by a mis-ranking function.

**4. The forward-model arithmetic is disqualifying for rollout-based search.** At ~115ms/action, a single rollout of even 300 remaining engine actions costs ~35 seconds; MCTS needs hundreds to thousands of iterations per decision. Matching even botbowl's *failing* 361-iterations-in-5s configuration would need roughly a 2,000x faster simulator. The standard escape is a second, stripped-down abstract forward model (Puppet Search evaluates scripts in an abstract state representation) — but that is a large build with a permanent correctness tax: every divergence between the abstract model and the real engine silently corrupts search results. What survives the arithmetic is bounded expectimax that never steps the engine: enumerate K candidate high-level options, score each with closed-form dice math and a cheap opponent-response model (1.5-2 ply). That is affordable — but it is exactly "greedy + eval," i.e., a better version of what you already have, and its ceiling is set by the eval.

**5. Published work on miniature wargames specifically: essentially none at your scale.** I found no peer-reviewed paper on full-rules Warhammer 40k AI. The visible attempts are hobbyist: a Towards Data Science series ("Training an AI to play Warhammer 40k") that explicitly strips deployment, stratagems, attack allocation, and psychic rules, and whose Part One contains zero results (design doc only); a Kill Team RL/genetic-algorithm YouTube project on a reduced variant; LLM-generated tabletop strategy experiments on hobby blogs (interesting precedent for LLM-authored *plans*, but anecdotal). Kriegspiel MCTS work exists but is about imperfect information on a chess board. The commercial precedent is the opposite of search: Wesnoth ships a candidate-action rule AI (its one ML success, a recruiter, beat the shipped AI 66-73% — on one sub-decision, with offline training); XCOM ships behavior-tree/utility scoring and manufactures challenge through scenario design plus *hidden dice modification in the player's favor* on lower difficulties; Into the Breach solved "tactics AI feels fair and challenging" by redesigning the game to be deterministic and fully telegraphed, with a simple scoring AI (Matthew Davis's published AI document). The reported industry claim that Total War: Rome II used MCTS for campaign AI (via the Świechowski MCTS survey) concerns a far more abstract decision layer than per-model tactics. The lesson: no shipped or published system achieves "challenging for experienced humans" in this genre through online search; they achieve it through design asymmetry, scripted competence, or (botbowl's MimicBot) imitation-of-scripts + RL.

**6. Opening books and pre-authored plans: the strongest precedent in the file.** Chess engines have used opening books for fifty years because search is weakest exactly where branching is highest and evaluation shallowest — the opening. StarCraft competition bots overwhelmingly ran hardcoded build orders, some literally mined from Liquipedia (Ontañón et al.'s 2013 survey; LetaBot), because the opening is a quasi-single-player optimization where offline human/LLM knowledge beats online search. Your measured facts — deployment "largely decides rounds 1-2," AI deployment doubles outcome sd, and your single biggest win (+27 VP) was a strategic reserves rule — map exactly onto this precedent. Pre-authored, per-mission deployment templates and macro game plans ("earmark these squads for the middle objective") are the one intervention here with both strong external precedent and supporting internal measurements.

**Verdict on the core question:** rollout-based search (MCTS/RHEA) against your real engine is DOA on arithmetic; script-space search (Puppet-style over your existing phase-plan knobs) is the only search family with literature support at "large map" scale, but its published wins are on millisecond-forward-model toys and are modest — treat as a speculative research project, not a roadmap item. Shallow closed-form expectimax on small discrete sub-decisions (charge declarations, fight sequencing, target allocation) is affordable and could fix specific blunders, but the TD-Gammon/diminishing-returns literature and your own null results both say the binding constraint is evaluation quality and macro/strategic policy — which is also where your only measured win came from.

## Key findings

- [reported] Vanilla MCTS collapses at multi-action board-game scale well below 40k's size: in Blood Bowl (11 pieces, discrete grid, ~10^50 turn branching) MCTS with 5s/decision achieved only a 10% win rate against a purely random opponent on the full board (361 iterations/decision), versus 95% on a 1-player toy board.
  Evidence: Justesen et al. 2019 challenge paper (branching ~10^50, 'breakdown of standard tree search methods, including MCTS'); official botbowl MCTS tutorial table: board size 11 → 10% wins, 0.1 TDs, 361 iterations at 5s/decision vs random.
- [reported] Years of open Blood Bowl competition never produced a search-based winner: scripted GrodBot won Bot Bowl I; the MCTS entry (Dryad) placed 3rd in Bot Bowl III; the first bot to beat scripted bots (MimicBot) worked by imitation-learning a scripted bot then RL fine-tuning. But well-built scripted bots beat random 10-0 — the genre's failure mode is 'can't beat scripted', not 'loses to random'.
  Evidence: Bot Bowl I/III official results pages; MimicBot paper (arXiv:2108.09478): 'no machine learning approach can beat a scripted bot which makes use of domain knowledge' prior to MimicBot; GrodBot 10/10 wins, 3.5 TDs avg vs random.
- [reported] The RTS field's answer to huge multi-unit turns is searching over scripts/abstractions, not raw actions (NaiveMCTS/CMAB, Puppet Search, asymmetric abstraction, Elastic MCTS) — and every published win is on microRTS/Stratega-scale toys with millisecond-or-faster C++ forward models; nothing at 40k rules complexity has been demonstrated.
  Evidence: Ontañón CMAB (AIIDE 2013/arXiv:1710.04805) evaluated on microRTS 8x8-16x16; Puppet Search (TCIAIG 2017) 'outperforming on larger scenarios' — in microRTS; Yang & Ontañón IEEE ToG 2021 survey; Elastic MCTS (arXiv:2205.15126) beats MCTS baselines with 10x smaller trees — in Stratega; Stratega explicitly built in C++ 'to assure a high execution speed' for forward planning.
- [reported] Even within script-space search, measured gains over greedy are small: Nested-Greedy (plain greedy over action abstractions) is competitive with adversarial search methods in the flagship microRTS domain.
  Evidence: Moraes et al., 'Nested-Greedy Search for Adversarial Real-Time Games' (AAAI/AIIDE), which motivates itself by showing greedy-over-abstraction rivals search-based methods.
- [inference] Rollout-based search against the real 40k engine is arithmetically dead: at ~115ms/action, one 300-action rollout costs ~35s; reaching even botbowl's failing 361-iterations-in-5s would require roughly a 2,000x faster simulator, i.e., building and maintaining a second abstract forward model with permanent desync risk.
  Evidence: 115ms/action is the project's own measured figure; 300 x 0.115s = 34.5s/rollout; botbowl reference point of 361 iterations in 5s = ~14ms per iteration including tree ops.
- [reported] In high-stochasticity games the proven pattern is strong evaluation + very shallow (1-3 ply) expectimax, not depth: TD-Gammon reached expert level at 1-ply and superhuman with 2-3 ply on top of a strong learned eval; chance nodes limit useful depth (backgammon effective branching ~400); and the value of added depth depends on evaluation quality (diminishing returns literature).
  Evidence: Tesauro TD-Gammon (CACM 1995; bkgm.com article); Hauk & Buro '*-Minimax Performance in Backgammon'; Junghanns & Schaeffer 'Diminishing Returns for Additional Search in Chess' (1997); Jones 'Scaling Scaling Laws with Board Games' (arXiv:2104.03113) — test-time search compute substitutes for train-time compute only given a trained evaluator.
- [inference] 40k's dice are analytically tractable (binomial hit/wound/save chains, 2D6 charges), so a 1-2 ply expectimax can use closed-form distributions — no determinization, no chance-node sampling, no cheating needed. Stochasticity handling is NOT the blocker for this project; forward-model cost and evaluation quality are.
  Evidence: Structure of the 40k rules (fixed success thresholds per roll); standard practice in stochastic-game search literature of exact chance-node expectation when distributions are closed-form.
- [verified] No peer-reviewed work exists on full-rules Warhammer 40k AI; visible attempts are hobbyist projects on heavily reduced variants (deployment, stratagems, attack allocation stripped) that published no results, and Kill Team RL toys.
  Evidence: Towards Data Science 'Training an AI to play Warhammer 40k: Part One' explicitly strips those rules and contains zero performance metrics; searches for academic 40k/miniature-wargame MCTS papers return Kriegspiel, Hex, and defense-wargaming material instead.
- [reported] Shipped commercial AIs in this genre (Wesnoth, XCOM, Into the Breach) are rule/utility-based with no online search, and manufacture challenge via scenario asymmetry, hidden player-favoring dice adjustment, or full determinism + telegraphing — never via search strength.
  Evidence: Wesnoth RCA candidate-action AI (wiki); XCOM 2 aim-assist config (documented multipliers, e.g., displayed 30% is actually 36-51% for the player on easy); Into the Breach AI document by Matthew Davis + design interviews on deterministic telegraphed attacks.
- [reported] Pre-authored openings are the single best-precedented technique for the user's plan: chess opening books are universal; StarCraft competition bots ran hardcoded build orders, some mined directly from Liquipedia; Wesnoth's one ML success (recruiter, +66-73% vs shipped AI) was an offline-trained sub-decision, not online search.
  Evidence: Ontañón et al. 2013 StarCraft survey; satirist.org StarCraft bot catalog (LetaBot mines Liquipedia); Wesnoth ML Recruiter wiki page with 66-73% win rates.

## Implications

Concrete implications for the 40k Godot project: (1) Do NOT build MCTS/RHEA/rollout search against the real engine — at 115ms/action the arithmetic fails by ~3 orders of magnitude, and the only escape (a second fast abstract simulator) is a large project with permanent desync risk and no literature evidence of payoff at this rules complexity. (2) The only search family with any at-scale support is Puppet-style plan-space search — and your architecture is already 90% of a Puppet agent: the phase plan (objective assignments, focus-fire matrix, charge intents) IS the script layer. If you ever test search, test 2-3 exposed choice points at the plan level (e.g., commit-vs-screen, which flank, reserves timing) scored by closed-form VP projections, not per-model search. Budget it as a research bet with your A/B lab as the gate, expecting a null. (3) Shallow closed-form expectimax (1.5-2 ply, exact dice math, no engine stepping) is affordable and worth trying ONLY on small discrete sub-decisions where blunders are visible — charge declarations (2D6 tail risk vs overwatch), fight sequencing, melee pile-in order — not on movement. (4) Your measured facts (damage-math fixes changed nothing, CEM null, 50% dominated moves, reserves rule = +27 VP) match the literature's central lesson exactly: evaluation quality and strategic/macro policy are the binding constraints, and search amplifies whatever evaluator you give it — adding depth to the current eval would likely reproduce your CEM null at 10-100x cost. Fix movement candidate generation/scoring first; it is a 1-ply defect. (5) The user's pre-authored deployment templates + per-mission game plans idea has the strongest precedent of anything considered (chess books, SC build orders) and directly attacks your two biggest measured levers (deployment decides rounds 1-2; strategic rules produced the only big win). LLMs are best used offline to author/compile these plans, not at move time. (6) Calibrate the goal: nothing published in this game class reaches 'challenging for experienced humans' without RL at scale (needs a fast simulator you don't have) — but 'challenging for novice/intermediate players' via a competent scripted engine + authored openings + modest force asymmetry (extra points, not dice cheating) is exactly what the genre's shipped games achieve, and is a realistic target.
