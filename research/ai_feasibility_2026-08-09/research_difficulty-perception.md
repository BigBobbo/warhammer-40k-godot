# research:difficulty-perception

## Narrative

"At least challenging" is a perception target, and the literature on perceived game-AI competence is unusually consistent — and unusually good news for this project's constraints, with one hard warning about its measurement lab.

**1. Players judge AI by legibility and worst visible blunder, not by strength.** The canonical sources are the Halo GDC 2002 paper (Butcher & Griesemer, "The Illusion of Intelligence") and Lars Lidén's "Artificial Stupidity" chapter. Bungie's playtest finding is the single most-cited datum in this field: when they gave the AI *more health* — no behavioral change — players rated it as *smarter*, because tougher enemies lived long enough to display their behaviors. Perceived intelligence tracked toughness, legibility, and telegraphed intent, not decision quality. Lidén's inversion is the other half: the designer's real job is tuning artificial *stupidity* so the player can win "without [the AI] looking unintelligent" — visible competence portfolio management, not Elo. F.E.A.R.'s famous GDC 2006 talk (Orkin, "Three States and a Plan") credits its reputation to visible re-planning and squad chatter that *announces* intent, so players attribute intelligence to what is actually a small planner. Academic believability work (Frontiers 2021 believability-assessment protocol; the CHI 2020 "Bot or not?" study; a 2024 level-generation study where players' *beliefs* about whether content was human-made drove enjoyment more than the truth) all point the same way: the experienced quality of an artificial opponent is dominated by attribution, not measured strength. I could not locate any GDC talk called "the Doppelganger effect" — that specific term did not surface in searches; the doppelganger literature that exists is about uncanny-valley avatars, not opponent AI. Treat that phrase as unverifiable; the Halo/Lidén/F.E.A.R. sources carry the same argument with receipts.

**2. Blunder elimination beats added cleverness — with a caveat about what "measures" it.** The negative space confirms this: what communities remember about bad strategy AI is its dumbest visible act, not its average play. TV Tropes' Artificial Stupidity catalog is a museum of single memorable blunders (Master of Magic trading a 1200-point Great Drake for a 45-point Hell Hound; Civ AIs voting to end wars they started while winning them). The bulk of Civ 6 AI criticism is about *visible nonsense* — contradictory agendas, tantrum diplomacy, never acting to stop the winning player — not about tactical depth. Age of Empires 2 DE is the constructive proof: its non-cheating "Extreme" AI is estimated by the community at only ~800–1000 Elo — objectively weak versus ladder humans — yet it reads as a serious opponent for the majority of players because the overhaul removed idiocy (counters your composition, uses siege correctly, micros groups) rather than adding search. This maps directly onto this project's measured "~50% of movement decisions chose destinations both farther and lower-scoring": that is precisely the blunder class (units drifting away from objectives) that defines perceived stupidity in this genre. Critical caveat, my inference: the project's lab measures *VP margin*, and the project has already proven (damage-math fixes → zero decisions changed → zero VP) that decision-quality changes can be invisible in VP. Perceived competence will be *equally or more* invisible in VP. The lab is currently blind to the actual target variable. But blunder-rate is measurable with far better statistical power than VP: a per-decision "was this dominated / did a unit idle / was an objective left uncontested at scoring?" audit yields hundreds of samples per game instead of one noisy VP number with sd 11–20. The existing per-decision Parquet/DuckDB records make this almost free to build.

**3. The thumbs-on-scale menu is community-legitimate almost all the way up — everything except dice.** Soren Johnson's "Our Cheatin' Hearts" (verified, fetched) draws the line precisely: "Cheating is not the same thing as difficulty levels"; player-selected, gradual, understood bonuses are accepted ("transparency is the antidote to suspicion and distrust"), while sudden visible cheats are rejected — and in symmetrical games "artificial intelligence often needs to cheat just to be able to compete." The 40k-adjacent digital games confirm tolerance in this exact audience: Gladius openly gives the AI a difficulty-indexed loyalty/production bonus (+15 to +50), the developer called it "a necessary evil," and players largely defend it *because it's opt-in and documented*; Battlesector's hardest difficulty is naked stat cheats (+⅓ HP, +15 accuracy, player capped at 80% points) and the dominant complaint is that lower difficulties are *too easy*, not that the cheats exist. Tabletop culture already practices asymmetric points: forum threads treat 1,000 vs 1,500 as a playable handicap, and organized-play comp systems have weaker factions bring 2,200–2,600 points against 2,000. So the ranked menu by acceptance: (1) points handicap / better AI list — fully legitimate, it's what humans do; (2) transparent stat or resource bonuses at player-chosen difficulty — accepted per Civ/Gladius precedent; (3) first-turn choice, favorable secondaries — accepted, these are in-rules asymmetries; (4) full information — in matched-play 40k, army lists are open information at tournaments, so an AI that "reads your list" violates no community norm (inference from tournament norms, but a safe one); (5) pre-authored deployments and plans — invisible and unobjectionable; (X) dice fudging and rules-breaking — rejected. One rich irony from the literature: the only widely accepted hidden dice manipulation in the genre runs *pro-player* (XCOM secretly inflates your hit chances below Legendary — Solomon: an 85% shot is really ~95% on easier settings), and Sid Meier's GDC 2010 keynote showed players find *honest* probability emotionally unacceptable (streaky 2:1 losses read as broken, so he bent the math). Consequence for a no-fudging AI: honest dice will periodically be *perceived* as cheating; the defense is Johnson's transparency — show every roll and the odds (the project's dice log already does this).

**4. Paper automas prove a designed, zero-compute opponent can pressure humans — and their structure is exactly the user's 'pre-described plan' idea.** Games Workshop publishes no official 40k solo automa (community systems and Crusade adaptations fill the gap), but the adjacent ecosystems are mature. Five Parsecs From Home — the most successful solo miniatures game — runs enemies on one-letter persona codes (Aggressive, Cautious, Defensive, Guardian, Rampage, Tactical, Beast), each a short condition→action list with a random-table fallback, and the solo community finds it genuinely challenging. One Page Rules ships official Solo/Co-Op AI rules for Grimdark Future (assigned unit roles + randomized activation + tables; updated Oct 2025). Board-game automas are the most engineered: Automa Factory's stated principles (Scythe, 300+ external playtests) are (a) simulate *only* the interactions that touch the human, (b) never make the human decide for the automa, (c) streamline ruthlessly; Glen More II's automa draws cards against a priority list and *shifts its priorities after each scoring* to reinforce where it leads and stay unpredictable, with five difficulty levels implemented as VP/resource handicaps — never as "smarter" logic; Root's Clockwork bots (community "Better Bot Project" turned official) are per-faction if-then priority stacks tuned until they score at human-like intervals. The transferable structure: persona/plan drawn at setup (variety), condition-ordered priority lists per unit role (competence floor, no dominated actions possible because the list only contains sensible actions), randomness only inside sensible options, and difficulty scaled by open handicap knobs. This is architecturally what the project already half-owns (phase plans, rule DSL, 238 parameters) — the automa lesson is to *author* the strategy layer per mission/deployment instead of searching for it, which is exactly where the project's only big measured win came from (reserves cap: 12→39 VP, a strategic pre-decision, not a tactical evaluation).

**5. Scoping: the opponent must pressure one hobbyist repeatedly, and the literature says variety-with-legibility, not strength, is what survives repetition.** Halo's AI endures because it is *legible* — players learn the Covenant's rules in an hour and enjoy probing them for years; playstyle-persona research (e.g. the 2025 "Playstyle and Artificial Intelligence" blueprint) frames recognizable decision-trait bundles as the mechanism for agent diversity and replay interest; automa design solves the same problem with priority-shift decks. And Bot Bowl — the user's own fear — actually *supports* feasibility on two counts I must state precisely: first, the random baseline never won a single game in Bot Bowl I (it scraped 7 draws, one against the champion — and Blood Bowl's low-scoring structure makes draws cheap, so "bots lose to random" overstates the record); second, scripted domain-knowledge bots (GrodBot, later Drefsante in 2022) beat ML approaches for years, with only MimicBot's imitation+RL hybrid eventually surpassing scripted play. In this genre — dice-heavy, huge branching, tabletop-derived — well-authored scripted play *is* the competitive frontier for small teams. The project does not need to solve what botbowl couldn't; it needs a non-blundering, persona-varied, transparently handicappable scripted opponent, which is the one thing every strand of evidence above says players actually experience as "challenging."

## Key findings

- [reported] Bungie's Halo playtests found players rated the AI as smarter when it was merely given more health, because it survived long enough to display behaviors — perceived intelligence tracks toughness and legibility, not decision quality.
  Evidence: GDC 2002 'The Illusion of Intelligence: The Integration of AI and Level Design in Halo' (Butcher & Griesemer); multiple secondary writeups: 'In extensive playtests, players confirmed that simply making the AI stronger helps it appear more intelligent.'
- [reported] Designer literature (Lidén's 'Artificial Stupidity') holds that the craft is tuning visible mistakes so the player can win 'without [the AI] looking unintelligent' — the worst visible blunder, not average strength, sets perceived competence.
  Evidence: Lidén chapter in AI Game Programming Wisdom / Rouse's Game Design: Theory and Practice; TV Tropes Artificial Stupidity catalog is organized entirely around single memorable blunders (e.g. Master of Magic trading a 1200-RP Great Drake for a 45-RP Hell Hound).
- [verified] Soren Johnson's rule for legitimate AI advantages: player-selected, gradual, transparent bonuses are accepted; sudden visible cheats are not; and in symmetrical games the AI usually needs bonuses to compete.
  Evidence: Fetched 'Game AI & Our Cheatin' Hearts' verbatim: 'Cheating is not the same thing as difficulty levels'; 'transparency is the antidote to suspicion and distrust'; 'artificial intelligence often needs to cheat just to be able to compete'; 'When the question is one of fairness, the player is always right.'
- [reported] Honest dice will sometimes be perceived as unfair: players systematically misjudge probability, and the industry's only accepted hidden dice-fudging runs in the player's favor.
  Evidence: Sid Meier GDC 2010 keynote (players revolt at streaky honest 2:1 losses; he bent the math); Jake Solomon interviews: XCOM below-Legendary multiplies displayed hit chances (85% shown is ~95% real; Rookie multiplies aim by 1.2).
- [reported] Bot Bowl's record supports, not refutes, feasibility: the random baseline never won a game (7 draws in Bot Bowl I), and scripted domain-knowledge bots beat ML entrants for years (GrodBot won Bot Bowl I; scripted Drefsante won 2022; only MimicBot's imitation+RL finally surpassed scripted).
  Evidence: Official Bot Bowl pages (njustesen.github.io/botbowl): 'the baseline random AI lost all games except 7, and those ended in a Draw'; botbowl docs/papers.md: 'currently no machine learning approach can beat a scripted bot which makes use of the domain knowledge'.
- [reported] A non-cheating AI of objectively modest strength can read as seriously challenging if it simply doesn't blunder: AoE2 DE's no-cheat Extreme AI is community-estimated at only ~800-1000 Elo yet is the accepted hard mode, built by removing idiocy (unit counters, correct siege use, group micro), not by search.
  Evidence: AoE2 DE forums/Steam discussions; AI overhaul notes (update 81058 added human-like group micro on Extreme); community Elo estimates 800-1000 for Extreme, 600-800 for Hardest.
- [reported] The 40k-adjacent digital audience tolerates open stat handicaps: Gladius gives difficulty-indexed loyalty bonuses (+15/+30/+50) that the dev called 'a necessary evil' and players defend as opt-in; Battlesector's hardest mode is naked cheats (+1/3 HP, +15 accuracy, player capped at 80% points) and the dominant complaint is lower difficulties being too easy.
  Evidence: Steam discussions for both titles; Gladius difficulty table (Trivial -15 ... Impossible +50 loyalty); Battlesector 'Higher difficulty levels...' thread.
- [inference] Tabletop culture already plays with points handicaps and tiered comp, so 'AI plays 2200 vs your 2000' is community-legitimate difficulty, not cheating; and in matched-play 40k army lists are open information, so full list-reading by the AI breaks no norm.
  Evidence: Heresy-online threads (1000 vs 1500 'playable', 3000 vs 2000 as outer limit); Old World comp systems letting weak factions bring 2200-2600 vs 2000. Open-lists claim is from tournament norms (inference, not a fetched citation).
- [verified] Paper automas make challenging opponents with zero compute using exactly the structure the user proposed: persona/plan drawn at setup, condition-ordered priority lists per unit role, randomness only among sensible options, difficulty scaled by open handicap knobs rather than smarter logic.
  Evidence: Automa Factory principles (simulate only player-touching interactions; never ask the player to decide for the automa; Scythe took 300+ external playtests); Glen More II automa (fetched): card deck + priorities that shift after each scoring, five difficulty levels; Five Parsecs AI codes (Aggressive/Cautious/Defensive/Guardian/Rampage/Tactical/Beast = condition→action lists with random fallback); OPR official Solo/Co-Op rules (unit roles + tables, updated Oct 2025); Root Clockwork = community if-then bots turned official. GW publishes no official 40k automa.
- [reported] For repeated play against one person, legible variety (personas) sustains challenge better than raw strength: Halo's legibility kept it interesting for years; playstyle-persona research and automa priority-shift decks both exist to prevent predictability, not to raise Elo.
  Evidence: Halo GDC talk legacy commentary ('not the smartest, but the most legible'); arXiv 2508.19152 'Playstyle and Artificial Intelligence' (decision-trait bundles create recognizable, diverse agents); Glen More II priority-shift mechanism explicitly framed as predictability prevention.
- [verified] No GDC talk called 'the Doppelganger effect' could be located; the doppelganger literature that surfaced concerns uncanny-valley avatars, not opponent AI. The load-bearing sources for the perception argument are Halo/Lidén/F.E.A.R./believability-protocol papers.
  Evidence: Two targeted searches returned only avatar/uncanny-valley doppelganger research (e.g. ResearchGate 'Face the Uncanny') and generic believability studies (Frontiers 2021 protocol; CHI 2020 'Bot or not?').
- [inference] The project's own VP-margin lab cannot measure the actual target ('feels challenging'): decision-quality changes provably invisible in VP (damage-math fix changed zero decisions) are exactly the changes that dominate perceived competence, while per-decision blunder-rate offers hundreds of samples per game versus one VP number with sd 11-20.
  Evidence: Project's measured results (damage-math corrections ~zero VP effect; CEM +2.25±3.2 VP over 264 games) combined with the perception literature above; the statistical-power argument is arithmetic on the project's own numbers.

## Implications

Concrete redirection for the 40k Godot project. (1) Change the target metric before changing the AI: add a blunder-rate harness on top of the existing per-decision Parquet/DuckDB records — classify each decision against a small checklist (dominated destination, unit idle, objective uncontested at scoring, charge declared then unreachable, shooting a strictly worse target with an obviously better one in range) and drive each class toward zero. This has per-decision statistical power (hundreds of samples per game), so it escapes the sd-11-20 VP trap that killed CEM and the YouTube-findings experiments; the measured 50% dominated-movement rate is the single highest-yield perceived-competence fix available, even if its VP delta measures near zero. (2) Build difficulty as a transparent handicap menu, not smarter code: AI plays 2100/2200/2300 vs your 2000, gets a curated stronger list, takes first turn, and reads your list (open info in tournament 40k) — all community-legitimate per tabletop handicap practice and Gladius/Battlesector precedent, and per Bungie's finding extra durability will itself be read as intelligence. Reserve any hidden bonuses for never; keep dice honest but keep the roll log prominent, because Meier/XCOM show honest dice get accused of cheating. (3) Author the strategy layer like an automa instead of searching for it: per-mission/per-deployment pre-authored plans (deployment templates, reserves policy, objective earmarks) drawn from 3-5 legible personas at setup, with the existing rule DSL as the condition→action priority-list substrate — the project's one big measured win (reserves cap, 12→39 VP) was exactly this class of pre-decision, and Five Parsecs/Scythe/Root prove persona+priority-list opponents feel challenging at zero compute. (4) Do not invest in search/rollouts/ML: Bot Bowl shows scripted domain knowledge is the competitive frontier for this genre at this team size, and the random-baseline scare is a misreading (random never won a game). Feasibility verdict from this evidence: making the AI feel challenging to one hobbyist owner is achievable with the assets already built — but only if effort moves from VP-optimized cleverness to blunder elimination, transparent handicaps, and authored persona plans, with perception (blunder-rate, and periodic human/LLM review of replays) as the primary gate.
