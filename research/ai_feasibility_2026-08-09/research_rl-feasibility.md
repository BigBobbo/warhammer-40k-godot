# research:rl-feasibility

## Narrative

## RL / self-play feasibility for the 40k Godot AI — evidence-first assessment

### 1. What RL actually cost in comparable games

The successes are all compute stories. AlphaGo Zero played 4.9 million self-play games in 3 days (verified from Nature-paper coverage) — generation ran on a TPU fleet; AlphaZero used 5,000 first-gen TPUs for self-play plus 64 TPUv2 for training over 700k optimizer steps (verified from the arXiv paper). OpenAI Five consumed ~45,000 years of Dota self-play over 10 months (~250 years/day) and 800 petaflop/s-days (verified, OpenAI). AlphaStar bootstrapped from ~1M human replays via supervised learning before league RL (verified, DeepMind). Even TD-Gammon — a tiny, fully-observable game with ~20 legal moves per position — needed ~1.5M self-play games (reported; standard literature figure, not re-verified). Every one of these had a simulator running orders of magnitude faster than real time.

The directly comparable case is botbowl (Blood Bowl), and it is a five-year controlled negative result. The Justesen et al. CoG 2019 paper (I extracted the PDF text) measures Blood Bowl's turn-wise branching factor at ~10^30 for random play (step-wise 29.8, 10.6 actions/turn) and ~4.8×10^51 for scripted play (17.2^41.6), vs ~30 for chess and ~300 for Go, with ~1,600 steps/game and reward only at game end. Their A2C baseline needed reward shaping (11 shaped reward terms) to learn anything even on cut-down boards; on the full 11-player board, vanilla A2C after **100 million environment steps** scored ~0.02 touchdowns/game — essentially nothing — and only curriculum learning (progressively shrinking an enlarged endzone) reached ~80% win rate **against a random opponent** at the same budget (verified from botbowl docs). Competition history (verified from official pages): Bot Bowl I — scripted GrodBot won; Bot Bowl II — scripted Minigrod won (47W/10D/3L) while the best RL entry, Gotebot (A2C + self-play, built on Google's SEED RL), went 26W/9D/25L, and two other ML entries finished **at or below the random baseline** (Sapling 1W/20D/39L, Giaobot 0W/20D/40L vs Random's 0W/23D/37L) — the user's fear is literally in the standings table; Bot Bowl III — the one ML win, MimicBot, which **imitated a scripted bot**, then RL-fine-tuned, and kept scripted rules in a hybrid decision process (its author states prior pure-RL attempts "failed... due to the intrinsic randomness in the environment and the large and uneven number of actions"); Bot Bowl IV and V — scripted Drefsante won both, beating ML entries including forward-model search bots (Treeman won the ML prize in V, not the tournament). Summary: in the closest published analogue to this project — dice-based, sparse-reward, huge uneven action space — scripted bots won 4 of 5 competitions, the single ML winner was scripted-bot imitation plus a scripted hybrid layer, and this was with a framework whose environment is fast enough to run 100M-step training on academic hardware.

### 2. The simulator arithmetic at 4 cores

Measured in-repo (tools/ai_lab/README.md): a 5-round 2000pt mirror game is ~196 decisions / ~751 actions; the Custodes mirror resolves in ~48 s headless, the Ork mirror in ~487 s; the lab's own rule is max 3 concurrent lanes on 4 cores. Throughput is therefore 3×86,400/487 ≈ **530 games/day** (Ork) to 3×86,400/48 ≈ **5,400 games/day** (Custodes-only best case) at 100% saturation — consistent with the stated 300–1,500/day mixed. Call it ~10^3/day, ~3–5×10^5/year if the machine does nothing else, ever.

Against the sample bars: AlphaGo Zero's 4.9M games = **2.5–25 years** of saturation. TD-Gammon's 1.5M games ≈ 1–8 years — for a game vastly simpler than 40k. The Blood Bowl curriculum-A2C budget (100M env steps ≈ 133k games at 751 actions/game) = **25–250 days** of total machine saturation, and the prize at that budget, in an easier game, was "80% vs random" — a bar the current heuristic already clears by construction. There is also a second, underrated wall: **evaluation**. With per-game VP-margin sd of 11–20 (measured), the project's own CEM run needed 264 games to get a ±3.2 VP interval. Deep RL produces hundreds of checkpoints per run; measuring any one of them to ±3 VP costs 2 hours–2 days of the same 4 cores. You cannot even afford to find out whether an RL run worked.

Sample-efficient methods do not rescue this. EfficientZero reaches 194.3% mean human on Atari-100k with 100k env steps (~130 of this project's games — superficially tantalizing) but (verified): it costs 7 h on 4 GPUs per 100k-step run (>40 GPU-hours), and the benchmark is **single-agent, dense-reward, 18 discrete actions, stationary environment**. There is no published result of 100k-step model-based RL in a two-player zero-sum game with 10^30+ branching; MuZero-class methods learn their world model from hundreds of millions of frames, and learning a surrogate of a dice-heavy 40k rules engine from ~10^2–10^3 games has no precedent (inference, but a safe one). DreamerV3 has the same world-model data problem. Offline RL (CQL-family) from the archived records is nominally in-range on transitions (1,000 games × 751 actions ≈ 750k transitions) but the reward is one terminal margin per game, so the effective sample count for credit assignment is ~the game count (10^2–10^3), from a **single behavior policy** — and any learned policy improvement still has to be verified against the ±3 VP evaluation wall. Behavior cloning from humans is dead for lack of data (no human-play corpus exists for this fan game); behavior cloning from the AI's own records (which are ideal BC data — candidates, scores, choice) reproduces the current bot minus fidelity, adding speed, not strength.

### 3. Continuous placement / action representation

The literature answer is parameterized action spaces (Hausknecht & Stone 2016 for Half-Field Offense; P-DQN/hybrid actor-critic follow-ups): a discrete action head plus continuous parameter heads, or discretized target points — AlphaStar used spatial pointer heads over the map grid. All demonstrated in fast simulators with heavy compute. For 40k, moving 5–20 units × 5–20 models each with coherency constraints makes even a discretized version (say 100 candidate destinations/unit) a ~100^10 ≈ 10^20 per-movement-phase space (inference). The practical route everyone in this genre actually uses — including this project already — is **candidate generation + ranking**: the heuristic proposes discrete destinations and something scores them. That reframing means "RL for movement" collapses to "a better ranker over generated candidates," which is a supervised/value-function problem, not a policy-gradient one. Note also the measured finding that ~50% of movement decisions chose destinations both farther and lower-scoring than an available alternative — the bot is not even taking its own argmax; no learned ranker is needed to fix that.

### 4. The learned value function (the only live option)

Precedent for small-sample learned evals exists and is specific: KnightCap went 1650→2150 Elo in **308 games** over 3 days (verified) — but with a *linear* eval over hand-crafted features, updated by TDLeaf inside deep alpha-beta search over a microsecond-fast forward model, playing **online against diverse humans**; the authors explicitly credit online play over self-play. Logistello and TD-Gammon similarly coupled learned evals to fast search. None of those preconditions except hand-crafted features currently hold here: the engine steps at ~60–600 ms/action, so any search deeper than "score afterstates with cheap math" is unaffordable at play time or training time. That is exactly why the repo's planned C1 (read from .llm/ai-overhaul-todo.md) is the right shape: a decomposed, deterministic evaluate_board() shipped *dark*, validated by Spearman correlation of round-3 V_diff vs final margin ≥ 0.5 on ≥100 archived games, with a kill criterion at <0.35. The *learned* upgrade that is realistic at this scale: fit the **weights** of that term decomposition (linear or small GBDT over ~20–100 hand-crafted features) as a final-margin regressor on round-end snapshots — ~10 labeled snapshots/game, effective N ≈ game count because outcomes are shared within a game, so 500–2,000 archived games support ~10–50 fitted parameters comfortably (inference from standard sample/parameter norms; KnightCap is the precedent that linear-over-good-features learns from hundreds of games). A deep net on 10^3 correlated games will overfit; do not build one. The consumer must be 1–2 ply search over the *abstracted* forward model (the planned expected-value combat math), never engine rollouts: 20 candidates × 200 decisions × real engine stepping would multiply game time ~20× (inference from measured action cost).

The honest caveat from the project's own data: two damage-math corrections measured ~zero because macro terms dominate target selection, the CEM sweep over 126 tunable weights found nothing outside noise, and the one large measured effect (+27 primary VP) came from a strategic-layer rule change. A fitted V is likely to encode the same macro signals the hand-written terms already carry; its expected marginal gain is unproven and plausibly small. It is worth doing because it is cheap (days of compute, existing records pipeline) and because search needs *some* leaf evaluator — not because the evidence says learning is where the wins are. The evidence says the wins are in authored strategic priors (deployment plans, reserves, objective stances) selected by measurement — which is the user's "pre-described game plans" idea, and it is the best-supported direction in the entire record.

## Implications

Dead on arrival at 4-core scale, with numbers: (1) end-to-end self-play deep RL for policy — the Blood-Bowl-calibrated budget (100M steps ≈ 133k games ≈ 25–250 days of total machine saturation) buys a beats-random-grade agent in an easier game, and botbowl's five competitions show pure RL finishing at/below random while scripted wins; (2) MuZero/DreamerV3 world-model methods — no precedent for learning a dice-heavy rules-engine surrogate from the 10^2–10^3 games this machine can produce; (3) offline RL (CQL-family) on the archived records — 750k transitions but only ~10^3 terminal-reward labels from a single behavior policy, and every off-policy improvement claim still pays the ±3 VP / 264-game evaluation toll; (4) behavior cloning from humans — no data exists. Marginal, not strength-bearing: BC/distillation of the AI's own records (speed only), and a learned candidate-ranker distilled from abstract search (only meaningful after the cheap EV forward model exists, at which point shipping the search directly is simpler). The one realistic learning-flavored item: keep C1 exactly as specified, and as its second iteration fit the term WEIGHTS (linear or small GBDT, ~10–50 parameters, never a deep net) as a final-margin regressor on 500–2,000 archived games — about 1–14 days of lane compute using the existing records pipeline — gated by the already-written Spearman ≥0.5 / kill-at-0.35 criterion, and consumed only by 1–2 ply search over the abstracted expected-value forward model (engine-rollout search multiplies game cost ~20× and is unaffordable). Expected gain is honestly unknown and plausibly small, because the project's own data says macro/strategic terms dominate. Therefore the resource-allocation answer: put the learning budget behind authored strategic priors — a library of pre-scripted deployment plans, reserves manifests, and per-mission objective stances selected by the paired evaluator — because that is the only channel with a measured +27 VP effect, it needs tens-to-hundreds of games per comparison rather than millions, and it is precisely the MimicBot lesson: in this genre, encoded domain knowledge beats learned policy at any compute the project can afford. RL as a route to 'challenging without cheating' is not the answer here; scripted strategy + shallow search over cheap math + (optionally) a small fitted value function is.
